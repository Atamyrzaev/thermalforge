      How RISC3D Studio works

      1. Real 3-D heat physics. The package — silicon interposer, microbumps in underfill, silicon die — is divided into a 3-D grid of cells (19,200 for a 20 × 20 mm package), and heat conduction through every cell is solved, cooled through the die backside. Each result is checked before it is shown: the equations are satisfied, and every watt generated leaves through the cooled face.
      2. Live floorplanning. Drag a chiplet and the whole package is solved again in milliseconds: the expensive matrix factorization is computed once per package and reused.
      3. Chiplets are not uniform heaters. An NPU's MAC array, a GPU's engines and a CPU's core complex carry most of the power over part of the die, and the junction follows the hot region. Pick what is inside each part in the editor: on an 8 W NPU, assuming uniform power under-reports the junction by 13 K.
      4. Only buildable layouts. Chiplets must clear each other by the assembly keep-out (100 µm by default) so underfill can flow and the bump dispense has room, so the optimizer cannot win by packing parts wall to wall.
      5. Exact optimization, not guessing. Heat adds up linearly, so any layout's temperature is the sum of what each chiplet causes on its own. The optimizer covers every layout on the placement grid and skips only branches it can prove are hotter than the best one found — a chiplet can only add heat. The winner is the provably coolest layout on that grid, and it is solved again from scratch before you see it.

      How we know it is right. Every solve checks its own residual and energy balance. The optimizer is checked against an independent brute force. The solver reproduces two closed-form solutions — a uniformly heated slab, to second order with the coefficient the discretization predicts, and a rectangular flux channel to within 3 %. The ± printed beside every peak is measured, not assumed: a 2 / 1 / 0.5 mm mesh convergence study plus conductivity perturbations (silicon ±10 %, bump composite ±30 %), added in quadrature. On these scenarios it comes to about 3.5 % of the rise above ambient, and the silicon conductivity dominates it — not the mesh.

      Scope: steady state; cooling represented by an effective heat-transfer coefficient on the die backside; power entering at the active plane of each die but spread over one 20 µm cell; uniform power inside each chiplet. All of that is verification — the model has not yet been compared with measurements of real hardware.

      Steady-state 3-D heat conduction, finite-volume solver, every number solved and checked (residual and energy balance) · peaks carry a measured mesh error bar · placement optimum is exact over the chosen grid · model verified, not yet validated against measurements
